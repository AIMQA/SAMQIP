Five minutes

Four things the whole method rests on

Model for Improvement

01 · THREE QUESTIONS

Ask these before anything else

  1. What are we trying to accomplish? One aim, with a number and a date: from [X] to [Y] by [when].
  2. How will we know a change is an improvement? One outcome measure, one process measure, one balancing measure — and a baseline before you change anything.
  3. What change can we make that will result in improvement? Sort your ideas on a quick 2×2 (impact against ease) and pick one quick win for cycle 1.

That is the Model for Improvement (Langley and colleagues, The Improvement Guide) — the framework behind the Roadmap, IHI, NHS QSIR and almost every course on the ladder below.

In the kitSMART aim builder for question 1 · the Workbook’s Data Plan tab for question 2 · the Diagram Tool’s 2×2 for question 3.
02 · ONE PDSA LOOP

Test small, then go round again

  • Plan — write your prediction down before you launch. “Completion rises from 60% to 80% within four weeks.”
  • Do — one change, one ward, a dated start. If it needs a committee, it is too big for cycle 1.
  • Study — compare what happened with what you predicted, on the run chart, not from memory.
  • Act — keep, tweak or drop. Cycle 2 should be smaller and sharper than cycle 1.

One loop of the Roadmap is one PDSA cycle: Stages 1–3 are Plan, 4 is Do, 5 is Study, 6–7 are Act. A first QIP with two honest cycles beats one big-bang change every time.

In the kitThe Workbook’s PDSA Log, or the PDSA worksheet — one or the other, not both.
03 · READING A RUN CHART

Know a signal from noise

  • Plot every week, baseline and after, with an arrow at the change. Draw the median of the baseline points.
  • A shift — six or more consecutive points on one side of the median — is a real change.
  • A trend — five or more consecutive points all rising or all falling — is a real change.
  • One dip is not a signal. Neither is one good week. Wait for 6–8 post-change points before the team decides — six is also the smallest run that can show a shift.

Those rules come from Perla, Provost and Murray (BMJ Quality & Safety 2011) and need no statistics. When you want control limits as well, the Run chart / SPC sheet draws them for you.

In the kitThe Workbook’s Data Log draws a plain run chart; RES-07 adds the median, shift signals and moving-range limits.
04 · QI, AUDIT OR RESEARCH?

Settle the label, then count — don’t name

  • Quality improvement — “how do we improve how we do it here?” You change something and measure the effect locally. Register with your trust’s clinical audit or QI office. No ethics committee.
  • Clinical audit — “are we meeting the standard?” Measuring against an agreed standard with no change being tested. Register with the audit team.
  • Research — “what should best practice be?” New, generalisable knowledge. Needs HRA / ethics approval before you start — speak to the R&D office.

Not sure? The HRA decision tool settles it in five clicks. Whichever label applies: no patient-identifiable data in workbooks, e-mails or on personal devices. If you genuinely need identifiers, use trust systems only and ask your IG team first.

In the kitThe Audit data template is the one file built for patient-level data — which is why it stays on NHS systems.
05 · WHO SPEAKS FOR PATIENTS?

Even one conversation counts

Ask a patient or carer what the problem looks like from the bed — one conversation, or a PALS or friends-and-family theme, is enough for a first project, and it changes what you choose to fix. If your unit wants a proper patient-reported measure, SAM already runs one: SAM-PLER collects patient-reported experience in acute medicine and gives you a measure you didn’t have to invent.

Go further

A ladder, not a syllabus

Free first, then SAM’s own courses

Nothing here is dated; course dates and booking live on SAM’s own pages so this page never goes stale.

1

Start with the kit 30 minutes · free

The Roadmap and the primer above. That is enough to pitch, plan and run a first project — most people never need more than this and the people around them.

2

Learn the method properly, at your own pace free

The NES Quality Improvement Zone has four free learner pathways — Kickstart QI, QI Essentials, Practical QI and Managing QI — open to anyone with an NHS or NES login, and its tools library is the best free one there is. HQIP’s Guide to Quality Improvement Methods explains ten methods, when each fits, and when it doesn’t.

The RCEM Quality Improvement Guide was written for emergency medicine trainees and supervisors and is still the best short practical guide in print. TIPSQI is trainee-led QI guidance and training for NHS doctors.

3

SAM’s own courses with Aqua

Acute Medics as Improvers — fundamental QI skills for resident doctors, ACPs and PAs in acute medicine, built around Same Day Emergency Care and delivered with the Advancing Quality Alliance. Acute Medics Leading Improvement — the advanced programme for those leading change. Both, with current dates and booking, are on SAM’s quality improvement page, which also covers QI certification.

If you want a large catalogue with certificates, IHI Open School has thirty-plus self-paced courses on improvement and safety (subscription; many trusts hold a licence — ask your QI team).

4

Write it up so it travels stage 6

SQUIRE 2.0 is the reporting standard; the kit’s Reporting Guide and Presentation Template walk you through all eighteen items. Finished projects can go to the Acute Medicine journal or BMJ Open Quality, which exists for exactly this kind of work; East London’s QI team has a practical guide to publishing QI work, written from a decade of doing it. And send it to the kit-format shelf of SAM’s Improvement Exchange on the hub; work in any other format goes through the Exchange’s own form.

5

Lead stage 7 and beyond

Own the next cycle, then a unit QI team, then a regional one. When you want to shape the national picture, email quality.improvement@acutemedicine.org.uk with the subject “Joining the SAM QIP team” — that, and submitting a finished project, are the two things the SAM inbox is for.

WHAT ASSESSORS ACTUALLY VALUE

The panel is looking for your engagement, not your slides

  • A run chart with baseline and post-change points — not a before-and-after bar chart.
  • More than one PDSA cycle, with the prediction written before each.
  • Honest reporting: what didn’t work, the dip in week three, what you changed.
  • A team — nurses, pharmacy, the ward clerk — not a solo hero.
  • A SQUIRE-shaped write-up and evidence you shared it: a QI meeting, a poster, the SAM conference.
  • A hand-over: the next cycle has an owner, even if it isn’t you.
WHY QIPs FALL SHORT

The same reasons, every year

  • No baseline — you can’t show improvement from nothing.
  • An audit with no change made, or a change with no re-measurement.
  • A proposal or a plan presented as a project.
  • Too big for one rotation, so it never finishes.
  • No diary, so nobody can explain what happened around the data.
  • Identifiable data in a workbook or an e-mail.
  • Attending QI teaching offered as evidence of doing QI.
  • Left to the week before ARCP, with no time for the QIPAT.

FAQ

The questions people actually ask

Is my project audit or QI?

If you are changing something and measuring the effect locally, it is QI. If you are only measuring practice against a standard, it is audit — and a repeat audit with no change in between is still audit. Register QI with your trust’s clinical audit or QI office; it does not need an ethics committee. Not sure? The HRA decision tool tells you whether it is research.

Do I need ethics approval?

Not for QI or audit. You do need to register the project locally, and you do need to follow information-governance rules — count, don’t name; no identifiable data outside trust systems. Research needs HRA approval before you start, so settle the label first.

Can I use SAMBA data as my baseline?

Often, yes. SAMBA gives participating units a report against national averages on severity, speed of assessment and outcomes — if your unit takes part, ask your SAMBA lead for your unit’s figures before you count anything yourself. You will still need weekly local counts to run the chart forward.

I rotate before it’s finished. Does it still count?

Yes — if you hand it over properly. Stage 7 of the Roadmap is the hand-over: pass the data plan, the diary and the run chart to a named person with a start date for cycle 2. A properly handed-over QIP is a completed stage, not an unfinished project, and the Workbook’s Share & Replicate tab is the one-page pack for it.

How many data points do I need?

A baseline of 10–20 cases a week for a few weeks, then 6–8 weekly points after the change before the team decides keep, tweak or drop. Twenty sets of notes is enough to start. More points beat more cases per point: the run chart needs time, not size.

I’m a nurse, pharmacist, therapist or ACP. Is this for me?

Yes. The kit is built for the whole multidisciplinary team, the Team Workbook expects a mixed team on its Home tab, and SAM’s Acute Medics as Improvers course is open to ACPs and PAs as well as doctors. Only the ARCP section on this page is doctor-specific.

How many QIPs should I have done?

The curriculum sets the floor — QI is one option for annual governance evidence at ST4–ST6, and one QIPAT-assessed project at ST7. SAM suggests aiming higher: be part of one as a medical student, complete at least one in Foundation, one more in core training, and two in higher specialty training — so the ST7 project is the fourth you finish, not the first. Guidance, not a rule.

Is there protected time for QI?

Yes, for AIM higher specialty trainees. The curriculum requires on average one day a week of self-development time, and the Specialty Advisory Committee has settled that this means 30 full days over 52 weeks — pro rata over a longer period if you train less than full time — with annual leave, study leave, industrial action and on-call not counting against it, split equally between acute medicine and specialty-block placements. It is for your specialist skill and point-of-care ultrasound first; once those are done or not chosen, it is explicitly for quality improvement projects, portfolio preparation and governance, leadership, research and education. Log what each day went on — unused or unevidenced time is dealt with under the Gold Guide. The ARCP evidence checklist sets it out in a table.

Where do I get help?

Your unit’s QI team first, then your region — see where help comes from. The SAM inbox is for two things only: joining the SAM QIP team, or submitting a finished project for the Exchange’s kit-format shelf.
